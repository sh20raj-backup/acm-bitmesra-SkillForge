# Evidence-Based Web Research

## Mission

Produce useful answers that are traceable to trustworthy, relevant evidence. Research is not just collecting links: it means clarifying the question, identifying the strongest sources, verifying the important claims, reconciling contradictions, and communicating uncertainty honestly.

Use this skill when a user asks for research, current information, market or product comparisons, technical documentation reviews, policy or standards research, or factual writing that needs citations.

## Core rules

1. Browse when freshness, niche facts, current availability, or external verification materially affects correctness and browsing is available.
2. Start with the exact research question and define scope; don't browse randomly.
3. Prefer primary sources for the claims they directly establish.
4. Verify key claims with a second independent source when impact or uncertainty warrants it.
5. Distinguish when an event happened from when an article was published or updated.
6. Cite specific claims close to the relevant evidence; don't dump unsupported assertions beside a list of links.
7. Separate evidence, inference, estimate, opinion, and recommendation.
8. Never fabricate citations, quotes, dates, sources, measurements, or direct observations.
9. If a source is inaccessible, stale, ambiguous or only a search snippet, disclose the limitation and seek corroboration.
10. If browsing is unavailable, state the limitation and don't imply that current research was performed.

## 1. Frame the question

Before searching, identify:

- The main question and subquestions
- Geography, date range, version, population or market segment
- What decision the research should support
- Required depth, audience and output format
- Claims that need current verification
- What evidence would support or disconfirm the leading hypothesis

Turn broad questions into a small set of searchable subquestions. Avoid researching tangents unless they change the answer.

## 2. Search strategy

Use several targeted searches rather than repeatedly issuing the same broad query.

- Start with primary sources: official docs, standards, legislation, filings, datasets, research papers, changelogs, product release notes, repository code and direct statements.
- Search reputable independent sources to provide context, critical analysis and corroboration.
- For community sentiment or lived experience, use the actual community discussion source and identify anecdote as anecdote.
- Use date filters/recency for fast-changing subjects, but still inspect the actual publication and event dates.
- Search for contradictions, limitations, failure cases and credible alternatives—not just sources that agree with an early impression.
- Prefer exact, documented specifications over unsourced summaries.
- Follow relevant internal links and inspect the original source when possible.

## 3. Evaluate sources

Assess each source for:

- **Authority:** is the publisher responsible for, close to, or qualified to establish this fact?
- **Directness:** does it directly contain the evidence or merely repeat another source?
- **Freshness:** is the content current enough for this claim? Is the page updated without a clear date?
- **Methodology:** does it explain data collection, sample, definitions, limitations and uncertainty?
- **Independence:** are two sources genuinely independent, or are they repeating the same announcement?
- **Scope:** does the claim apply to the same version, country, time period or population?
- **Incentives:** could the source benefit from exaggeration or selective presentation?
- **Reproducibility:** can key numbers, dates or quotations be traced to the original material?

Do not rank a source as authoritative simply because it has a polished website or ranks highly in search.

## 4. Verify claims and numbers

Create a private evidence map while researching:

- Claim
- Best source and URL
- Relevant date/version
- Exact supporting passage, table, or data point
- Corroborating or conflicting source
- Confidence and qualification

For numerical claims:

- Check units, denominators, currencies, time zones and date range.
- Recalculate arithmetic or percentages where practical.
- Check whether values are rounded, forecast, reported, audited, sampled or estimated.
- Avoid comparing figures with different definitions without explaining that limitation.
- Preserve the original value and label any conversion or calculation.

For quoted claims, verify the exact wording on the source page. Quote only what is necessary and comply with applicable copyright limits.

## 5. Freshness-sensitive subjects

For current technology, product releases, public figures, laws, schedules, finance, health guidance or breaking news:

- Check a source with a publication or update date that fits the requested window.
- Verify whether the described event occurred earlier than the article.
- Check the latest official release note, policy, standard or schedule where applicable.
- Never treat the model's prior knowledge as confirmation of a current fact.
- Use exact dates instead of ambiguous relative wording in the final report.
- If the latest status can't be established, say what was last verified and what remains unknown.

## 6. Technical and product research

For software/library recommendations:

- Inspect official installation, versioning, API, migration and deprecation documentation.
- Distinguish a feature's existence from support in the user's installed version.
- Check maintenance status, license, compatibility, security advisories and integration constraints when material.
- Compare trade-offs, not just feature lists.
- Avoid claiming benchmarks unless the source describes setup and methodology.
- Prefer a small recommended stack to a long catalogue of overlapping tools.
- If implementation is requested, verify the actual API rather than copying obsolete snippets from secondary blogs.

## 7. Market and business research

- Define geography, customer segment, and the metric being compared.
- Separate total addressable market estimates from reachable customers and likely revenue.
- Prefer primary datasets, company filings, regulator publications and transparent studies.
- Distinguish company-reported results from independently audited or estimated values.
- Identify assumptions, uncertainty and sources of bias.
- Do not state that a product has no competition after a shallow search.
- For recommendations, include risks and disconfirming evidence as well as upside.

## 8. Build the final report

A strong report usually includes:

1. Direct conclusion or executive summary.
2. Key findings grouped by topic.
3. Comparison table or diagram where it makes the information easier to understand.
4. Evidence and inline citations near factual claims.
5. Methodology or scope note when the research is substantial.
6. Conflicts, uncertainty, limitations and counterevidence.
7. Practical next steps tied to the findings.
8. A short list of the most important sources when useful.

Adapt the format to the user’s request. Don't make every answer a long report. For a recommendation, make the recommendation clear and explain the decisive trade-offs.

## 9. Citation discipline

- Cite the source that supports each material externally derived claim.
- Link to the original or canonical page, not a search-results page if the original is available.
- A citation should substantiate the wording immediately around it.
- Use primary and corroborating sources together when that improves confidence.
- Do not use a source merely because it mentions the same keyword.
- If sources disagree, cite both and explain the basis for your judgment.
- Never cite a page that was not actually inspected when direct access is required to establish the claim.
- If the output format has a citation convention, follow it consistently.

## 10. Avoid common research failures

- Search-snippet-only conclusions
- Stale docs presented as current
- One-source claims for contested or high-impact facts
- Confusing correlation with causation
- Reporting market forecasts as measured facts
- Mixing units or incompatible time periods
- Invented citations and unsupported numbers
- Quoting too much text instead of summarizing
- Hiding uncertainty in confident prose
- Adding visualizations that obscure rather than explain the evidence

## Acceptance checklist

- [ ] Research question and scope are clear.
- [ ] Search strategy covers primary sources and credible alternatives.
- [ ] Material claims are supported by sources that directly establish them.
- [ ] Current claims use appropriate publication/event dates.
- [ ] Important numbers, units and calculations are checked.
- [ ] Conflicting evidence and uncertainty are stated.
- [ ] Citations are accurate and placed near the claims they support.
- [ ] Recommendations reflect trade-offs and scope, not unsupported certainty.
- [ ] No sources, quotes, dates or observed results were fabricated.
- [ ] The final answer matches the requested depth and audience.
